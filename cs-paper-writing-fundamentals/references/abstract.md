# Abstract

The abstract is often the only part a reader sees. It must stand alone.

## What it must contain
1. **Problem**: what is being solved, and why it is hard or important (1-2 sentences).
2. **Gap**: what existing approaches cannot do (can be folded into the problem).
3. **Approach**: what you did, at the level of the key idea, not the implementation.
4. **Result**: the main finding, with numbers or a precise qualitative statement.
5. **Significance**: what the result enables or changes.

## Rules
- Self-contained: no citations, no undefined symbols or acronyms, no references to figures or sections.
- Every claim in the abstract must be delivered in the body. If the body does not support it, cut it here.
- Prefer a concrete result ("reduces planning time by 3.2x on 40 benchmark scenes") to an adjective ("significantly faster").
- Do not open with a generic statement about the field ("Robots are increasingly used..."). Start where your problem starts.
- Do not describe the paper's structure ("Section 3 presents..."). Describe the work.
- Length: follow the venue limit; otherwise aim for 150-250 words in one paragraph.

## Check before finishing
- Could a reader outside your subfield say, from the abstract alone, what you did and what you found?
- Is each number traceable to a table or figure in the paper?
- Are the contribution verbs honest? ("We prove" only for proofs; "We show empirically" for experiments; "We propose" for designs.)
