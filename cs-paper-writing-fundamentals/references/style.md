# Style: Sentences, Paragraphs, Terminology

## Paragraphs
- One idea per paragraph; the first sentence states it.
- Each sentence should connect to the previous one by a visible relation: cause, contrast, consequence, refinement, or example. Add transition words where the relation is not obvious.
- Reverse-outline test: list the first sentences. They should tell the argument on their own.

## Sentences
- Prefer short, direct sentences and the active voice.
- Put the important information at the end of the sentence, where it gets emphasis; put known context at the start.
- Cut filler: "in order to" -> "to"; "it is the case that" -> delete; "very", "quite", "basically", "essentially" usually go.
- Avoid "obviously", "clearly", "trivially": if it is obvious the word is unnecessary; if not, it is condescending.
- Avoid ambiguous references: "this", "it", "they" need a clear antecedent. "This approach" beats "this".
- Do not start a sentence with a symbol or numeral.
- Avoid long noun stacks ("robot arm trajectory collision cost term"); break them up.

## Voice and person
- Use "we" for what the authors do or claim ("We prove..."). Do not use "we" for what a system or robot does, and avoid "we" as a stand-in for general truths.
- Tense: present for what the paper shows and for general facts; past for what was done in experiments.

## Terminology
- One concept, one name, throughout the paper, figures and captions. Do not vary terms for elegance.
- Define terms and acronyms at first use. Prefer plain words over invented jargon.
- Introduce an acronym only if it will be used several times.
- Keep notation consistent across text, equations, pseudocode and figures.

## Common problems to scan for
- Hedging stacks ("may possibly potentially").
- Vague quantifiers ("a lot of", "many") when a number is available.
- Claims in the passive that hide who did what ("it was found that").
- Paragraphs that start with a citation as the subject of the sentence rather than the idea.
